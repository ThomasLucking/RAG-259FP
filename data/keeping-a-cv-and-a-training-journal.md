## Two records, one purpose

Apprentices keep two personal records alongside their technical work: a CV
aimed at future employers, and a training journal aimed mostly at
themselves and their trainers. Both answer the same question later — what
have I actually done, and what can I prove about it — and neither is a
one-off document.

The two serve different audiences. A CV is a pitch: short and curated,
written for someone deciding whether to invite the apprentice to an
interview. A training journal is an archive: longer and exhaustive,
written for the apprentice's own memory and for anyone who later needs
evidence of what was learned. Confusing the two leads to a CV that reads
like a diary, or a journal that reads like marketing copy.

## What a developer CV needs to carry

Recruiters scan a CV quickly, so the layout must surface what matters most:
identity, contact details, a link to a code-hosting profile, and a short,
genuine paragraph on why the applicant is drawn to the field and to the
kind of company they are targeting. Generic or inflated statements stand
out immediately to an experienced reader and undermine trust in the rest
of the document.

The core of a developer CV is the project list: each entry names the
project, gives a one-line description, and lists the technologies
involved. Professional or internship experience follows, in reverse
chronological order and only kept where relevant to the target profession
— ordinary school history does not belong once qualifications are
established. Languages, personal strengths, hobbies framed around
transferable value, and references close out a document whose layout is
otherwise free, but that keeps changing throughout training as new skills
replace outdated ones.

## The training journal as a running habit

A training journal collects, project by project, what was built, in what
context, with which technologies, and what role the apprentice actually
played — full implementation, partial contribution, testing, or support,
together with evidence such as screenshots or links to a working demo or
repository.

The value of this record depends on when it is written. Reconstructing a
whole training period from memory at the end produces thin entries and
forgotten details; writing shortly after each project, while the context
is fresh, captures the real difficulties faced and keeps the journal ready
on short notice for a review, an interview, or a dispute over competencies.
Honesty matters as much as coverage: a stalled project or a skill only
partially acquired is worth recording as such, since the point is real
learning, not a flattering narrative. AI assistance is fine, but the
apprentice must stay able to explain every part of the content unaided.

## Plain files, kept under version control

Both documents are typically authored as plain source files — text or
lightweight markup rather than a fixed, closed format — and kept under
version control. This makes their history inspectable: earlier states stay
available and changes over time are visible, with nothing to export or
convert before sharing. Treating personal records the same way as project
code turns them into something durable, rather than documents rewritten
from scratch every time they are needed.
