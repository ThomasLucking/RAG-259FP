## Narrowing the subject and finding sources

A technical subject — a programming language, a tool, an architecture
style, a development practice — is almost always too wide for a short talk.
The first task is deciding which angles deserve airtime: origin and
context, core mechanics, typical use cases, trade-offs against
alternatives. A subject narrowed to a handful of angles, explored with
concrete detail, gives an audience something to retain; one covered
end-to-end tends to stay shallow everywhere.

Research then draws on several kinds of sources, each useful for a
different angle: official documentation for accuracy on how something
works, encyclopedic overviews for context and history, blog posts and
conference talks for practical or opinionated perspectives, and public code
repositories for evidence of real-world usage. None of these should be
trusted blindly — documentation can lag behind the latest release, blog
posts reflect one author's experience, and a project's own site emphasizes
strengths over weaknesses. Cross-checking a claim across independent
sources matters most for anything time-sensitive, since technical
ecosystems move fast and older material goes stale quickly.

## Adapting depth to the audience

The same subject is presented differently to peers who already work in the
domain than to an audience with no background in it. Presenting to fellow
apprentices usually means assuming basic programming knowledge but not
familiarity with the specific subject, which shapes how much foundational
explanation opens the talk versus how quickly it moves to comparison and
nuance.

Depth also interacts with time: a short slot forces choices about which
concepts get a full explanation, which get a one-line mention, and which
are cut entirely. Slides carry illustrations and key terms while the spoken
explanation carries the reasoning; the two should complement rather than
repeat each other.

## Individual work versus group presentations

A solo presentation puts one person in charge of the entire narrative — one
voice, one structure, no risk of contradicting a co-presenter — but all the
research, source-checking, and example-building falls on that person alone.

A group presentation usually covers a richer subject, split among several
people, which multiplies research capacity but introduces a coordination
problem: each member researches part independently, yet the audience should
hear one coherent story rather than loosely connected mini-talks. Agreeing
on a shared structure before splitting up the research, then reviewing each
other's sections, helps avoid overlap or gaps. Delivery adds a further
layer: transitions between speakers need planning, and speaking time should
be distributed evenly rather than concentrated on whoever feels confident.

## Handling questions afterward

A technical presentation typically ends with questions from the audience,
and answering them well requires understanding the subject beyond what
made it onto the slides. Anticipating a few likely follow-ups during
preparation — a limitation glossed over, a comparison left implicit — is
more useful than memorizing extra facts.

When a question falls outside what was researched, acknowledging the gap
is preferable to improvising an answer. In a group, deciding informally who
is best placed to take which kind of question, and letting that person
answer without interruption, keeps the closing exchange as coherent as the
rest of the presentation.
