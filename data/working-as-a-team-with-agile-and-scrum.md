Agile is a family of values and principles for building products under
uncertainty: short feedback loops, working software over exhaustive
documentation, and adaptation over rigid up-front planning. Scrum is the most
widely used framework that puts those values into practice. It does not
prescribe technical practices; it prescribes a small set of roles, artefacts,
and time-boxed events that structure how a team plans, works, and reflects.

## Roles

Scrum defines three accountabilities, not job titles. The Product Owner owns
the product backlog: writing and refining items, ordering them by value, and
deciding what "acceptable" means for each one. The Scrum Master is not a
manager; the role exists to make sure the events happen, to remove obstacles
that slow the team down, and to protect the group's ability to focus on its
current goal. Developers is the term Scrum uses for everyone who turns
backlog items into a usable product increment, whatever their specialty.

A team that rotates these roles usually discovers that each one changes how
the work looks from the inside. Owning the backlog forces trade-off
decisions; facilitating events surfaces friction that doers rarely notice
because they are busy doing.

## The backlog and the sprint

The product backlog is an ordered list of everything that might improve the
product, expressed as user stories or similar increments of value, each with
acceptance criteria that make "done" checkable rather than a matter of
opinion. A healthy backlog is never complete; it is refined continuously as
understanding of the product grows.

A sprint is a fixed, short time box - commonly one to four weeks - during
which the team builds a usable increment toward a sprint goal chosen at
Sprint Planning. The time box is not negotiable once it starts: scope can
flex, the deadline does not. This constraint is what makes progress
measurable and forces the team to slice work into small, shippable pieces
instead of one large effort finished only at the very end.

## The ceremonies

Each Scrum event answers a specific question, and skipping the question
while keeping the meeting defeats the purpose:

- Sprint Planning asks what can be delivered this sprint and how the team
  intends to get there; it produces the sprint goal.
- The Daily Scrum asks whether the team is still on track toward that goal
  and what is in the way; it is a synchronisation point for the team, not a
  status report to a manager.
- Sprint Review asks whether the increment is actually valuable; it is held
  with stakeholders and often includes a live demonstration, followed by an
  update to the backlog based on what was learned.
- Sprint Retrospective asks how the team's way of working can improve; it
  looks at process and collaboration, not at the product, and should end
  with concrete actions the team commits to trying next.

## Definition of done and mindset over ritual

The definition of done is a shared, explicit checklist describing what
"finished" means for any increment - for example, tested, reviewed, and
deployable. Without it, teams disagree silently about quality, and "done"
degrades into "written." The definition of done is agreed once for the
team and applied to every backlog item, while acceptance criteria are
specific to a single item.

Running the events and filling the roles is not the same as being agile.
A team can hold a Daily Scrum every day and still hide blockers, or run a
Retrospective and never act on it; at that point Scrum has become ritual
without substance. The agile mindset behind the framework is about
transparency, inspection, and adaptation: making real progress and real
problems visible, then actually changing behaviour based on what is
observed, sprint after sprint. Scrum only works as a container for that
mindset, not as a substitute for it.
