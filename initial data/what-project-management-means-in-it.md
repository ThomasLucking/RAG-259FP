## Why IT projects need managing at all

Writing software is not the same activity as delivering a software
project. A project adds constraints that pure technical work does
not have: a deadline, a budget, people who expect a specific result,
and other people who depend on that result for their own work.
Management exists to hold these constraints together while the
technical work happens underneath.

IT projects are also unusually hard to manage: the product stays
invisible until it runs, so progress is hard to observe directly,
and requirements shift as people see early results and understand
their own needs better. None of this disappears by working faster or
writing better code; it has to be managed.

## Scope, time, and cost, and the tension between them

Every project is bounded by three quantities: what has to be
delivered (scope), how long that can take (time), and what it can
consume in effort or money (cost). These interact. Adding scope
without adding time or people usually means missing the deadline, or
cutting quality to compensate. Shortening the timeline without
reducing scope tends to raise cost, since more people or overtime
are needed to compress the same work.

A project rarely gets to fix all three at once. Managing it means
choosing which is allowed to move when something goes wrong, instead
of pretending nothing has to give. A team that refuses to adjust any
of the three usually sacrifices quality instead, since nobody
negotiated on that dimension.

## Requirements, planning, and why estimates go wrong

Scope comes from requirements, and requirements come from
stakeholders: the people who will use the result, the people who pay
for it, and the people who maintain it afterwards. These groups
rarely want exactly the same thing, and their needs are often
incomplete or contradictory at the start. Reconciling their
expectations early keeps the team from building against a moving
target.

Planning breaks the agreed scope into pieces that can be scheduled
and estimated. Estimation in software is notoriously unreliable for
structural reasons, not for lack of skill: software has no physical
shape to inspect before it exists, so hidden complexity only
surfaces once work is under way, and people tend to estimate the
parts they can picture while forgetting integration, edge cases, and
the rework the first attempt usually needs. Estimates made earliest,
when least is known, are the least reliable and yet the most likely
to be treated as fixed promises. This is one reason iterative
approaches replan often instead of trusting a single early estimate
for the whole project.

## Risk, tracking progress, and the classic causes of failure

Risk is anything that could happen and derail the project: a key
person leaving, a dependency that does not behave as documented, a
misunderstanding about what "done" means. Managing risk does not
mean avoiding every uncertainty; it means identifying which ones
matter most and deciding in advance how the team would react.

Following progress is how a team notices drift before it becomes a
crisis. It requires an honest measure of what is actually finished,
not what is in progress or "almost done," since partially completed
work tends to hide the riskiest remaining parts. A project that
checks progress against the calendar alone, without checking real
completion, tends to look on track for a long time and then suddenly
not be.

Most IT project failures trace back to a small set of recurring
causes: requirements left unclear or allowed to expand without the
schedule adjusting to match, stakeholders left out until late,
estimates treated as fixed commitments, and risks known about but
never acted on. None of these causes are purely technical, which is
why managing a project is a distinct activity from writing the
software itself.
