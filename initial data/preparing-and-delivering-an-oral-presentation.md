## Knowing the audience and the objective

A presentation is aimed at a specific group of people with a specific
outcome in mind, so preparation starts with the audience and the goal.

### Reading the audience

Before drafting a slide, it helps to know who is in the room, what they
already know, and what they expect. Those answers decide the vocabulary,
the detail level, and the examples that will land.

### Setting a clear objective

Every talk fits one of three broad goals: informing, persuading, or
motivating. Stating it in one sentence keeps every later choice, from
structure to slides, aligned with it.

## Structuring the talk

### Opening with a real hook

A plain self-introduction wastes the one moment attention is guaranteed.
A stronger opening leads with a question, a striking figure, or an
anecdote, then states the topic and the plan.

### Building the body

Three to five main ideas is the practical ceiling; beyond that the
audience loses the thread. Each works well as a claim, an explanation,
and a concrete example, linked by explicit transitions. A short
narrative — situation, problem, journey, resolution — tends to stick
better than a plain list of facts, and a change of pace every ten
minutes counters the drop in attention that follows passive listening.

### Closing with intention

"That's all I have" undercuts everything said before it. A deliberate
ending signals the wrap-up, restates the key points briefly, repeats the
one message meant to be remembered, then opens the floor to questions.

## Delivering with voice and body

### Voice, pace and pauses

Volume should fit the room, pace should stay moderate, and articulation
should stay clear under nerves. A silence after a strong statement lets
it sink in, and a pause in place of a filler word reads as composure.

### Body language and eye contact

An upright, still posture, facing the audience rather than the screen,
reads as confidence. Sweeping the room with the eyes, rather than fixing
on one person, keeps the audience engaged, and gestures that match the
words reinforce the message.

### Managing stage fright

Nervousness before speaking is close to universal, and its physical
signs overlap with excitement. Framing the sensation as excitement
rather than anxiety loosens its grip; rehearsal remains the most
reliable antidote.

## Slides, rehearsal and feedback

### What a slide is for

An audience processes a talk through two limited channels, visual and
auditory; text that repeats the spoken words saturates both at once.
Working memory holds only a handful of elements at a time, so a slide
with one idea, in sync with the words, works better than a crowded one.

### Rehearsing deliberately

Confidence on stage is mostly the product of repetition: a mental
run-through to fix the flow, a spoken one for pacing, a recorded one for
non-verbal habits, and a final one in front of others.

### Using feedback to improve

Vague feedback ("it was good") rarely helps. Specific questions — was
the structure clear, was the pace right — turn one talk into material
for the next, and tracking feedback across several talks is what
actually drives improvement.
